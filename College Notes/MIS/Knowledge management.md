## 1. Steps involved in knowledge capturing

**Knowledge capturing** is the process of identifying, collecting, documenting, organizing, and storing valuable knowledge from employees, documents, customers, systems, and business experience so that it can be reused by the organization. It includes both **explicit knowledge**—such as manuals, reports, and procedures—and **tacit knowledge**—such as experience, skills, judgement, and know-how.[1][2]

### Introduction

Every organization creates knowledge through employee experience, customer interactions, research, projects, mistakes, and daily operations. If this knowledge is not captured, it may remain only in the minds of individual employees and can be lost when they leave, retire, or change roles.

Knowledge capturing is therefore the first major step in Knowledge Management (KM). Its purpose is to convert individual or informal knowledge into an organizational asset that other employees can access and use.[1][3]

### Step 1: Identify valuable knowledge

The organization first identifies which knowledge is important for business success. Not all information needs to be captured; the focus should be on knowledge that helps solve problems, improve decisions, train employees, or improve products and services.

**Activities:**

- Identify critical business processes.
- Identify experts and experienced employees.
- Identify frequently asked questions and common problems.
- Identify knowledge gaps.
- Decide which knowledge is useful for future reuse.

**Example:** A software company identifies that the process for handling a major server failure is valuable knowledge. It decides to capture the steps followed by senior engineers during previous incidents.

### Step 2: Locate knowledge sources

After identifying the required knowledge, the organization finds where it exists. Knowledge may be available in people’s minds, documents, databases, emails, customer complaints, project reports, training material, or recorded meetings.

**Knowledge sources:**

| Source | Type of knowledge | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Employees and experts | Tacit knowledge | A senior engineer’s troubleshooting experience |
| Manuals and reports | Explicit knowledge | User manual or project report |
| Customer feedback | Customer knowledge | Complaints, reviews, suggestions |
| Business systems | Operational knowledge | Sales data, service tickets, production records |
| Meetings and discussions | Team knowledge | Decisions and solutions discussed in meetings |
| External sources | Market knowledge | Competitor reports, research papers, industry news |

**Example:** In a hospital, knowledge sources may include doctors’ treatment experience, patient records, medical guidelines, laboratory reports, and feedback from nurses.

### Step 3: Collect and capture knowledge

In this step, the organization collects knowledge from the identified sources. Explicit knowledge can be collected from documents and systems, while tacit knowledge must be extracted through interviews, observation, discussions, and mentoring.

**Methods of capturing knowledge:**

- Interviews with experts.
- Observation of employees performing tasks.
- Group discussions and brainstorming sessions.
- Recording meetings, presentations, and training sessions.
- Reviewing documents, emails, reports, and case files.
- Analysing customer complaints and support tickets.
- Conducting exit interviews before employees leave.
- Using video recordings, screenshots, and demonstrations.
- Creating standard templates for employees to document their work.

**Example:** A senior technician explains how to repair a machine. The organization records a video, prepares a step-by-step repair manual, and adds common troubleshooting tips. This converts the technician’s tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge.

### Step 4: Validate and filter knowledge

Not all collected information is useful, accurate, or relevant. The organization must check whether the knowledge is correct, current, complete, and useful before storing it.

**Activities:**

- Remove duplicate or irrelevant information.
- Verify facts with subject-matter experts.
- Check whether the knowledge is outdated.
- Identify confidential or sensitive information.
- Select only knowledge that supports business objectives.

**Example:** A company collects 100 customer-service solutions. After review, it keeps 60 accurate and relevant solutions, removes duplicate answers, and updates solutions that refer to old software versions.

### Step 5: Codify and structure knowledge

**Codification** means converting captured knowledge into a standard, understandable, and reusable format. Tacit knowledge is converted into documents, flowcharts, videos, checklists, templates, or step-by-step procedures.

**Methods:**

- Prepare manuals and standard operating procedures (SOPs).
- Create FAQs.
- Develop flowcharts and process diagrams.
- Use templates and checklists.
- Create case studies and best-practice documents.
- Record videos or demonstrations.
- Use tags, categories, keywords, and metadata.

**Example:** A bank converts an experienced loan officer’s decision-making process into a loan-approval checklist, eligibility rules, and a flowchart. New employees can use this document to process loan applications.

### Step 6: Store knowledge in a knowledge repository

The validated and codified knowledge is stored in a central, searchable, and secure knowledge repository. This may be a knowledge base, intranet portal, document-management system, wiki, cloud storage, or knowledge-management software.

**Requirements of a good repository:**

- Easy search and retrieval.
- Proper categorization and tagging.
- Access control and security.
- Version control.
- Backup facilities.
- Clear ownership of documents.
- Regular updating.

**Example:** A company stores troubleshooting guides, customer FAQs, product manuals, and training videos in a central knowledge base. Employees can search for a solution using keywords such as “login problem” or “payment failure.”

### Step 7: Share and disseminate knowledge

Captured knowledge becomes useful only when employees can access and use it. The organization must share knowledge through training, collaboration tools, communities of practice, mentoring, newsletters, and internal portals.

**Methods of sharing:**

- Knowledge base and intranet.
- Training sessions and workshops.
- Mentoring and coaching.
- Communities of practice.
- Discussion forums and chat groups.
- Email alerts and newsletters.
- Video tutorials.
- Job rotation and shadowing.

**Example:** A new customer-support employee uses the company knowledge base to solve a common billing issue instead of waiting for a senior employee to explain it.

### Step 8: Apply and reuse knowledge

Employees apply captured knowledge while performing daily work, solving problems, training new staff, making decisions, and improving processes.

**Example:** A service engineer uses a stored troubleshooting guide to repair a machine. The solution is reused whenever a similar problem occurs, saving time and reducing errors.

### Step 9: Update and maintain knowledge

Knowledge becomes outdated as technology, market conditions, laws, products, and business processes change. Therefore, the organization must regularly review and update stored knowledge.

**Activities:**

- Review documents periodically.
- Update procedures after system changes.
- Remove obsolete knowledge.
- Add new solutions and lessons learned.
- Track document versions.
- Assign responsibility for maintenance.

**Example:** A software company updates its installation guide after releasing a new version of its application. Without updating, employees may follow old instructions and create errors.

### Knowledge-capturing flow

```text
Identify valuable knowledge
        ↓
Locate knowledge sources
        ↓
Collect and capture knowledge
        ↓
Validate and filter knowledge
        ↓
Codify and structure knowledge
        ↓
Store in knowledge repository
        ↓
Share with employees
        ↓
Apply and reuse knowledge
        ↓
Update and maintain knowledge
```

### Example: IT company

An IT company captures knowledge in the following way:

1. It identifies frequent software errors faced by customers.  
2. It collects solutions from support engineers, customer tickets, and technical documents.  
3. It interviews senior engineers to understand hidden troubleshooting methods.  
4. It validates the solutions and removes duplicate answers.  
5. It prepares FAQs, troubleshooting guides, and step-by-step resolution procedures.  
6. It stores them in a searchable knowledge base.  
7. It shares the knowledge base with support staff through training.  
8. Support employees use the stored solutions to solve customer issues quickly.  
9. The company updates the knowledge base whenever new software versions are released.  

### Conclusion

Knowledge capturing is a continuous process of identifying, collecting, validating, codifying, storing, sharing, applying, and updating organizational knowledge. Its main benefit is that valuable employee experience and business knowledge are preserved and made available to the entire organization, rather than being lost when employees leave.

## 2. Challenges faced by Knowledge Management in business scenarios

Knowledge Management faces challenges because knowledge is often scattered, personal, confidential, outdated, or difficult to express. The main challenges arise from people, processes, technology, and organizational culture.[4][5][6]

### Introduction

Knowledge Management aims to capture, store, share, and use organizational knowledge effectively. However, implementing KM is difficult because employees may not want to share knowledge, tacit knowledge may be hard to document, and information may be stored in separate departments or systems.

These challenges differ across business scenarios such as manufacturing, healthcare, IT, banking, education, and customer service.[4][7]

### Challenge 1: Capturing tacit knowledge

**Tacit knowledge** is knowledge that exists in a person’s mind, experience, intuition, skills, and judgement. It is difficult to write down or transfer to another person.

**Example:** A senior technician knows from experience that a machine makes a particular noise before failure. This knowledge may not be written in any manual.

**Business scenario:** In a manufacturing company, an experienced machine operator may know how to detect early signs of equipment failure. If the operator retires, this knowledge may be lost.

**Solution:** Use mentoring, shadowing, interviews, video demonstrations, communities of practice, and storytelling to capture tacit knowledge.[4][5]

### Challenge 2: Employee resistance to sharing knowledge

Some employees believe that sharing their knowledge may reduce their importance, job security, or competitive advantage within the organization. They may prefer to keep knowledge private.

**Example:** A senior salesperson may not share successful sales techniques because they fear that other employees will become equally capable.

**Business scenario:** In a consulting firm, senior consultants may hesitate to share client-solving methods because their expertise gives them professional value.

**Solution:** Create a knowledge-sharing culture, reward employees for contributing knowledge, recognize experts, and make knowledge sharing part of performance appraisal.

### Challenge 3: Knowledge silos

Knowledge silos occur when information remains within one department, team, or individual and is not shared with other parts of the organization. Departments may use different tools, databases, or communication channels, making collaboration difficult.[4][7]

**Example:** The sales department knows customer complaints, while the product-development department has technical information. If they do not share knowledge, product improvement may be delayed.

**Business scenario:** In a hospital, doctors, nurses, laboratory staff, and billing staff may maintain separate records. Lack of integration can delay treatment or create errors.

**Solution:** Use a centralized knowledge repository, integrated systems, cross-functional teams, and regular interdepartmental meetings.

### Challenge 4: Outdated knowledge

Knowledge becomes outdated due to changes in technology, laws, customer needs, products, and business processes. If employees use old information, they may make wrong decisions.

**Example:** A company’s troubleshooting guide refers to an old software version. A new employee follows the old steps and cannot solve the customer’s issue.

**Business scenario:** In banking, regulations and compliance rules change frequently. If the knowledge base is not updated, employees may give incorrect advice to customers.

**Solution:** Assign knowledge owners, conduct regular reviews, use version control, and remove obsolete documents.[1][8]

### Challenge 5: Lack of time and resources

Employees may be too busy with daily work to document their knowledge. Organizations may also lack budget, software, trained staff, or management support for KM initiatives.

**Example:** A support engineer may solve customer problems quickly but does not have time to write a detailed solution in the knowledge base.

**Business scenario:** In a small business, employees may handle multiple roles and have no dedicated KM team or software.

**Solution:** Provide simple templates, allocate specific time for documentation, automate knowledge capture from support tickets, and appoint knowledge champions.

### Challenge 6: Poor knowledge quality

Knowledge may be incomplete, inaccurate, duplicated, inconsistent, or poorly organized. Poor-quality knowledge reduces trust in the knowledge-management system.

**Example:** Two employees store different solutions for the same technical issue. A new employee does not know which solution is correct.

**Business scenario:** In a pharmaceutical company, incorrect knowledge about a drug dosage or manufacturing process can create serious safety and legal risks.

**Solution:** Validate knowledge with experts, use approval workflows, remove duplicates, and maintain quality standards.

### Challenge 7: Difficulty in measuring KM value

It is difficult to measure the direct financial benefit of knowledge management. Organizations may find it hard to prove whether KM has improved productivity, reduced costs, or increased revenue.

**Example:** A company introduces a knowledge base but cannot easily measure how much time employees saved by using it.

**Business scenario:** In a customer-service company, management may know that complaints are being resolved faster, but may not know whether the improvement is due to KM, new software, or better training.

**Solution:** Measure indicators such as reduced resolution time, fewer repeated questions, employee training time, customer satisfaction, and reduced error rates.

### Challenge 8: Security, privacy, and confidentiality

Some knowledge is sensitive and cannot be shared freely. Employee expertise, customer data, business strategies, research findings, and financial information must be protected.

**Example:** A company cannot share its product-design secrets with all employees or external partners.

**Business scenario:** In a hospital, patient records contain private medical information. KM systems must control who can access which records.

**Solution:** Use role-based access control, encryption, authentication, audit trails, and data-classification policies.

### Challenge 9: Lack of management support

Knowledge Management requires leadership support, funding, policies, and long-term commitment. If managers do not encourage knowledge sharing, employees may not participate.

**Example:** Management may buy KM software but not give employees time or incentives to use it.

**Business scenario:** In a manufacturing company, supervisors may focus only on production targets and ignore documentation of best practices.

**Solution:** Top management should communicate the importance of KM, provide resources, set KM goals, and include knowledge sharing in performance evaluation.

### Challenge 10: Technology and integration problems

Organizations may use many separate systems, such as email, spreadsheets, cloud storage, chat applications, ERP, CRM, and document-management systems. If these systems are not integrated, employees may not know where to find knowledge.

**Example:** Customer information may be stored in CRM, technical solutions in a knowledge base, and internal discussions in chat groups. Employees must search multiple systems.

**Business scenario:** In a retail company, sales, inventory, supplier, and customer data may be stored in separate systems, making it difficult to get a complete view.

**Solution:** Use integrated KM platforms, enterprise search, APIs, standardized formats, and centralized repositories.

### Challenges in different business scenarios

| Business scenario | Major KM challenge | Example | Suitable solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing | Loss of tacit knowledge from experienced workers | Machine operator knows early signs of failure | Mentoring, video demonstration, SOP |
| Healthcare | Privacy, accuracy, and knowledge silos | Patient information spread across departments | Integrated records, access control |
| IT/software | Rapidly changing technical knowledge | Old troubleshooting guide becomes outdated | Regular review and version control |
| Banking | Compliance and security | Employees need updated regulatory knowledge | Central compliance knowledge base |
| Education | Knowledge depends on teachers and experience | Teaching methods differ among faculty | Learning repositories, peer sharing |
| Customer service | Repeated questions and inconsistent answers | Agents give different answers to same issue | FAQ, chatbot, knowledge base |
| Consulting | Experts hesitate to share methods | Senior consultant keeps client-solving approach private | Rewards, communities of practice |
| Retail | Data and knowledge silos | Sales and inventory data stored separately | Integrated ERP/CRM and dashboards |

### Conclusion

The major challenges of Knowledge Management are capturing tacit knowledge, employee resistance, knowledge silos, outdated information, poor quality, lack of time, security concerns, and lack of management support. These challenges can be reduced through a knowledge-sharing culture, proper technology, employee training, incentives, centralized repositories, regular updates, and strong data-security controls.[4][5][6]
